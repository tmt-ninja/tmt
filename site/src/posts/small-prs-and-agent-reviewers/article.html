"Keep pull requests small" is one of the most repeated rules in software. It exists for a good reason: a person can review a few hundred lines with real attention, and a big PR gets skimmed. We carried that rule into tmt without thinking about it. Our agents opened a PR for almost every ticket, and many tickets were small.

But in tmt, agents write the code and agents review it. A squad lead agent reads every changed file, its callers and its tests, and it does not get tired at line 400. What stays expensive is the fixed cost of each PR: one review, one full set of CI runs, one agent watching the checks until they are green. If the reviewer is not the bottleneck, the old reason for small PRs may not hold, and how finely we split the work becomes an open question.

On 31 October the owner tested it with one rule: one member, one PR, carrying several tickets of the same module. Tickets may stay small; PRs may not. We compared 14 working days before the rule (17 to 30 October) with 9 full working days after it, in the first half of November.

Fewer PRs, and fuller ones

The change shows from the first day. Before the rule we merged about 79 PRs a day, and almost every one finished exactly one ticket or none. After it we merged about 29 a day, and PRs finishing two, three or four tickets appear: the red band.

As a distribution, the share of PRs finishing two or more tickets went from 1.4% to 17.1%, twelve times as many. The share finishing none rose too, from 25% to 35%: steps of a larger ticket marked "Part of", plus chores such as releases and daily stats.

A fifth fewer reviews per ticket

Every PR gets at least one review from its squad lead. Before the rule, finishing a ticket took 1.30 PRs, and so 1.30 reviews. After it, 1.02. That is 22% fewer reviews for the same finished work, and review is the most expensive thing a lead does: reading every changed file, its callers and its tests.

The squads that bundled most gained most. GUI, the busiest squad, went from 0.80 to 1.14 tickets per PR, Core from 0.70 to 0.84, and Night from 0.84 to 1.75. TUI went the other way: most of its PRs in these weeks were steps of larger tickets, marked "Part of" rather than "Fixes".

Per ticket, the fixed costs fell

Daily counts alone cannot tell a better process from a quieter fortnight, so we divided everything by finished tickets, the work that actually shipped. Two costs come once per PR, however many tickets it carries: the lead's review, and the merge-queue CI run that checks a PR against the latest main just before it lands. Both fell per ticket. The CI that runs on every push to an open PR did not: a bundled PR goes through more rounds of fixes before it is green.

lower per tickethigher per ticket

Watching CI got cheaper too. Agents used to watch CI by polling it in a loop, and every poll costs tokens. From 27 October a wait guard blocked sleep-polling, and from 1 November CI completion wakes the agent instead. Logging began with the guard, so we cannot see the days before it, but Codex CI polls ran at 200 to 665 a day from 27 to 31 October and fell to under 150 a day from 2 November on.

What comes next

CI per push. Bundling removed fixed costs, but all CI together stayed level per finished ticket: 19.9 runs before the rule, 20.6 after. Per 1,000 hand-written lines it fell slightly, from 28.0 runs to 26.2. CI a day halved, from 1,205 runs to 593, and so did the code we merged, from about 43,000 to 23,000 lines a day: CI kept pace with the work and did not outgrow it.

The reason is pushes. A PR now takes 20.3 runs instead of 15.2, because a bigger PR goes through more rounds of fixes before it is green, and every push runs the whole CI again. Bundling removed the fixed cost per PR; the cost per push is the next target.

Time to merge. The median PR now takes 80 minutes from opening to merge, up from 64. A bundled PR carries more: a median of 424 changed lines instead of 359, and a review that covers two or three tickets takes longer than one that covers a single ticket. Every PR in a squad waits behind the same lead, so longer reviews also lengthen the queue for small PRs. A quarter of an hour more per PR, for a fifth fewer reviews, is a trade we will keep.

We count reviews rather than tokens because our usage log does not separate a lead's review from the rest of its work. Fewer reviews per ticket is the saving we can measure directly.

What we take from it

  • Small PRs were made for human reviewers. With agent reviewers, in our first two weeks, the fixed cost of a PR mattered more than its size: per finished ticket, bundling cut reviews by 22% and merge-queue CI runs by 15%. Per 1,000 lines of code, reviews fell by 30%, and total CI did not grow faster than the work.
  • Bundle by module, not by count. The squads that gained most did it because their tickets touched the same files. Unrelated tickets bundled together only make a review harder, for an agent as for a person.
  • The right size is still open. We do not yet know where an agent reviewer starts to miss things, or how many pushes a large PR can absorb before CI eats the saving. Next we will count review findings and pushes per PR against PR size, and report what we find.
